Lesson 3 · Level B2

Second & Third Conditionals

Hypotheticals, regrets, and alternate timelines. Basically the grammar behind every multiverse movie you've watched in the last five years. Go get a coffee, this one's a marathon.

Why this is secretly the most "you" grammar topic

A rant, as always, before the actual content

Okay so think about how many movies and shows in the last few years are just... multiverse stuff. Spider-people from different dimensions. A woman jumping between lives where she made different choices at every possible fork in the road. A guy stuck in a time loop reliving the same day until he becomes a slightly less terrible person. A whole superhero universe built on "what if this one thing had gone differently." That's not a coincidence that this trend blew up right now — it's because "what if" is just an endlessly interesting question, and it turns out English built an entire grammar system just to ask it properly.

That system is called conditionals, and today we're doing the two that actually matter for how you think, not just how you talk: the Second Conditional (imaginary present/future — "if I were rich, I'd buy a house") and the Third Conditional (imaginary past — "if I had studied, I would have passed"). Then, because I like to torture you slightly, a small preview of Mixed Conditionals, which is what happens when the Second and Third have a baby.

The one-sentence version, if you read nothing else: Second Conditional = a made-up present or future that isn't true right now. Third Conditional = a made-up past that can never be true, because that moment already happened and it happened differently. One is "imagine if," the other is "if only."

1. Quick recap: the conditional family

You already half-know the first two types. This is just so we're all speaking the same language before we go deep on 2 and 3.

Type Structure Used for Example
Zero If + present, present General facts, things that are always true If you heat ice, it melts.
First If + present, will + verb Real, possible future situations If it rains, I'll stay home.
Second If + past simple, would + verb Imaginary present/future, unlikely or impossible right now If I had a time machine, I would fix so much.
Third If + past perfect, would have + past participle Imaginary past — the opposite of what actually happened If I had left earlier, I wouldn't have missed the bus.

Zero and First are about the real world — real habits, real possible futures. Second and Third leave the real world entirely. That's the whole shift you need to make in your head: with Second and Third Conditionals, you are, grammatically speaking, lying. On purpose. About a world that doesn't exist. That's the fun part.

2. The Second Conditional — your multiverse-self grammar

For the present or future that isn't real, isn't likely, or is straight up impossible

Part Form
If-clause If + subject + past simple
Result clause subject + would (or could/might) + base verb

Yes, the if-clause uses past simple even though we're talking about now or the future. That trips people up constantly — you're not describing something that happened, you're using the past tense to signal "this is unreal, this is distance from reality," which is honestly a very poetic thing for a grammar rule to do and nobody appreciates it enough.

Famous exception you'll see everywhere: with the verb "to be," B2-and-up English almost always uses were for every subject, even "I" and "he/she/it" — "If I were you," not "If I was you." You'll hear "was" in casual speech, but "were" is the one to actually use, especially in writing.

Think of it like this: every Second Conditional sentence is basically you narrating one specific alternate-universe version of your life. "If I lived in Tokyo, I would eat ramen every day" isn't a plan, it's not likely, it's just... a version of you, in a universe that isn't this one, living slightly differently. That multiverse-jumping character everyone loves in movies right now? She's basically speaking in Second Conditionals the entire time, just without saying "if."

Some real uses you'll actually need:

  • Giving advice: "If I were you, I would just text them back."
  • Daydreaming out loud: "If I won the lottery, I'd move somewhere with no winter."
  • Talking about something that's technically possible but you don't expect: "If aliens landed tomorrow, I would absolutely lose my mind."
  • Being polite/hypothetical instead of direct: "If you had more time, would you help me with this?"

3. The Third Conditional — the "if only" grammar

For a past that already happened a different way, and no amount of grammar can change that, sorry

Part Form
If-clause If + subject + past perfect (had + past participle)
Result clause subject + would have (or could have/might have) + past participle

This is the one for regret, for "what if," for replaying a moment in your head that you cannot change no matter how many times you replay it. Every single one of those alternate-timeline movies where a character sees the version of their life where they made one different choice — stayed with that person, took that job, said that one sentence they didn't say — that entire emotional concept is a Third Conditional. "If I had told her how I felt, things would have been different." That's it. That's the whole genre, grammatically.

The mistake basically everyone makes at this level: putting "would have" in BOTH halves of the sentence — "If I would have known, I would have come" — it feels natural because "would have" sounds strong and dramatic, but it's wrong. The if-clause needs past perfect (had known), not "would have known." Save "would have" for the result only.

Some real uses you'll actually need:

  • Regret: "If I had gone to bed earlier, I wouldn't have been so tired."
  • Explaining what almost happened: "If you hadn't grabbed my arm, I would have fallen."
  • Defending or excusing yourself: "If you had told me sooner, I would have helped."
  • Imagining a completely different life: "If I had never moved here, I would have never met half my friends."

4. Second vs. Third — the side-by-side that finally makes it click

Same "if only" energy, completely different timeline

Question to ask yourself Second Conditional Third Conditional
Is this about now/the future, or about something already finished? Now / future Already finished, in the past
Can this theoretically still happen? Maybe, in some version of events No — that specific moment is gone forever
If-clause tense past simple past perfect
Result-clause form would + base verb would have + past participle
Emotional flavor daydreaming, advice, "imagine if" regret, relief, "if only"

5. A quick taste of Mixed Conditionals

This is where it gets genuinely fun — we're not doing the full deep-dive today, that's closer to C1 territory, but you should at least recognize it

Sometimes a past decision has a result that's still true right now — not in the past, right now, today. That's a Mixed Conditional: past condition (Third-style if-clause), present result (Second-style result clause).

Structure Example What it means
If + past perfect, would + base verb If I hadn't dropped out of that class, I would have a completely different career now. The decision was in the past, but I'm feeling the effect of it today, right now, present tense.

Notice the if-clause looks exactly like a Third Conditional, but the result clause looks like a Second Conditional. It's the "that one decision explains a lot about my current life" sentence, and honestly it might be the most emotionally accurate grammar structure in the entire language.

6. Common mistakes, ranked by how often I've seen them

If you make one of these right now, that's fine, that's literally why this list exists

Wrong Right Why
If I would have more money, I would travel. If I had more money, I would travel. No "would" in the if-clause of a Second Conditional. Ever. It's a rule you just have to accept, like taxes.
If I would have known, I would have come. If I had known, I would have come. Same rule for Third Conditional — if-clause gets past perfect, not "would have."
If I was you, I would say sorry. If I were you, I would say sorry. Not wrong in casual speech, but "were" is the B2+/formal-writing standard for every subject with "to be."
If I will have time, I will call you. If I have time, I will call you. This one's actually First Conditional, not Second — but the mistake is the same family: no "will" in the if-clause, ever, in any conditional.
If she would have text back, I would have replied. If she had texted back, I would have replied. Also: it's "texted," not "text," in past participle. Small thing, still counts.

7. Your turn — pop-culture prompts

Don't write these down yet, just think about the sentence in your head. This is what the actual assignment for this lesson is going to be built on.

  • Second Conditional: think of any show or movie with alternate versions of the same character living completely different lives because of one different choice. Now make a sentence about your own life: "If I had chosen [a different city/school/job], I would probably be ___ right now." (That's actually a Mixed Conditional if you think about it, which — good, you're already ahead.)
  • Third Conditional: think about a breakup song, any breakup song, they're basically all the same — someone always wonders how things could have gone differently. Now write your own "if only" sentence about something small and low-stakes, like missing a bus or forgetting someone's birthday. Keep it low-stakes on purpose, save the emotional ones for later.
  • Mixed: pick one decision from your teenage years — school subject, hobby, a friendship you let fade — and describe, in one sentence, how it's still shaping your life today.

Full written version of this goes up on the Assignments page. For now just get comfortable with the shape of these sentences in your head — that's honestly 80% of the battle with conditionals.
